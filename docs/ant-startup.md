## 1. Objective and positioning

Present Ansilum as an AI-native POS for Indonesian micro, small, and medium businesses (UMKM). Show how ordinary point-of-sale activity becomes business context that an owner can consult through AI.

The central promise is: **Use your POS normally. Ask your business data what to do next.**

The customer problem extends beyond recording transactions. Owners need actionable insight from their sales and products. Connecting an AI service, preparing data, and writing effective prompts creates a barrier for UMKM. Ansilum's product direction brings that consultation into the POS workflow.

The redesigned homepage must communicate the customer, business problem, core workflow, development status, and next step within the first screen. Support the Claude Startups application with a coherent product story, a specific intended Claude integration, and a visible business history.

Write confidently and concretely. Use factual feature-status labels rather than tentative prose. The AI experience in this redesign is a product preview with sample data. Use one clear caption for the complete preview; keep the surrounding copy focused on the owner and the workflow.

### Brand story: AI belongs in everyday business

The founder's motivation is to extend useful AI tooling to Indonesian UMKM through familiar daily work. Tell a story about access, relevance, and confidence: an owner deserves tools that fit their business, answers based on their records, and room to learn without judgment.

Use **“small businesses underserved by current AI tools”** in the English brief. In Indonesian customer copy, explain the gap directly: **“Pemilik usaha membutuhkan alat AI yang mudah digunakan dan sesuai dengan pekerjaan sehari-hari.”** Describe the work required to connect tools, prepare records, and ask useful questions. Keep the owner's skill and knowledge of their business central to the story.

Do not call owners “laggards,” “late adopters,” “behind,” or “technologically illiterate.” Do not claim that only technology companies benefit from AI or that every owner experiences ridicule. Frame broader access as Ansilum's purpose. Treat making a poster or writing a product description as a valid way to start using AI; show how the same curiosity leads into understanding sales and choosing the next business step.

### Full story for About — English

AI should be as useful at a coffee counter, in a barbershop, or at a food stall as it is inside a technology company.

Ansilum started in March 2024. Our customers include coffee, barber, and dimsum businesses in Indonesia. Our next chapter is bringing AI into the daily work of UMKM, with the owner's own business records as its starting point.

A busy owner needs a straightforward way to begin. Learning how to connect an AI service, prepare sales records, and write the right question adds work. We want that first step to feel familiar: record a sale, ask about the business, and read an answer that points back to the records.

Making a poster, writing a product description, or asking about sales is a valid use of AI. Business owners deserve space to try useful tools and learn with confidence. The product must explain itself in everyday language and respect the owner's experience.

We are building toward a cashier app where owners ask what sold, what changed, and what needs attention. The consultation starts with sales and products, shows the period and records behind the answer, and suggests a practical next step. The owner brings their knowledge of the business and makes the decision.

Our purpose is to put useful AI within reach of everyday businesses in Indonesia: easy to start, connected to their records, and relevant to the work they do each day.

## 4. Customer and message hierarchy

Serve Indonesian UMKM first. Use cafés, barbershops, and small food businesses as concrete starting examples. The homepage must extend beyond cafés and restaurants, and it must not impose a 1–5 outlet restriction on the market positioning.

### Start from the owner's situation

Design for an owner searching for a better way to run the business. They know the daily friction: transactions keep coming, records accumulate, and deciding what to do next takes time. They do not need a fully formed AI specification to recognize the value of Ansilum.

Start with recognizable questions: “What sold most?”, “Where did sales change?”, and “What needs my attention today?” Explain the product through the answer to those questions. Define it in ordinary language: **a cashier app with an AI assistant that uses your sales records to answer questions about your business.** Keep “AI-native POS” as an internal positioning term; introduce the customer experience with familiar words.

Move the visitor through four stages: recognize their problem, see a useful answer, understand why the POS provides the context, and choose a demo. Explain the benefit before asking them to evaluate the technology.

Address two roles:

- **Owner or manager:** reviews sales, asks business questions, and decides the next action.
- **Cashier or operator:** records transactions and keeps operational data current.

### Explain why Ansilum

Build the rationale around the connected product workflow:

- **The records start where the work happens.** Sales and product activity enter through the POS the team uses every day. Show the relationship between a transaction and the consultation rather than treating AI as an unrelated chat window.
- **The owner starts with a business question.** Give them useful question suggestions and a natural-language conversation. The design removes the need to learn prompt formulas or choose an AI connector before understanding the product.
- **The answer refers to their business.** Show the selected business, date range, product breakdown, and supporting records. Explain how this context makes the consultation useful for an operational decision.
- **The conversation leads to a practical next step.** Pair the explanation with a specific review task, such as inspecting coffee sales by day. Let the owner move from the answer to the relevant records and a checklist.
- **The starting point is familiar.** Use Indonesian language, rupiah, local business examples, and everyday operating questions. Explain onboarding in terms of the owner's current workflow.

Present these as the rationale for the product design and demonstrate them in the labeled preview. Keep feature availability consistent with the established development status. Support differentiation with visible behavior, business context, and customer history; omit unsupported superiority claims.

Use this narrative order:

1. Run daily sales through the POS.
2. Build context from sales and product records.
3. Consult the AI in everyday language.
4. Inspect the explanation and supporting data.
5. Choose the next business action.

Use sales and products as the initial input story. Introduce stock, recipes, costs, and margin only with the corresponding product scope and data requirements. POS is the foundation of the consultation, and AI is central to the product direction.

## 11. Visual direction and what to avoid

### Make the page delightful and distinctive

Use the warm cream, coffee-brown, and charcoal palette as a foundation. Add saturated accents to highlight questions, changes in sales, and selected actions. Give the page a recognizable visual motif: a line that carries a sale through business context into a useful answer.

Create rhythm with large typography, asymmetric compositions, full-width color transitions, open editorial sections, and close-up product details. Alternate immersive product storytelling with quiet reading space. Broaden the imagery to include coffee shops, barber businesses, and small food businesses. Make the page feel inviting, energetic, and useful to an owner.

The connected POS and AI consultation is the visual centerpiece. Let interface fragments and the connecting line extend beyond a conventional screenshot frame. Preserve readable figures and a clear reading order. Use actual product screens where available and designed UI for the AI preview.

### Animation and creative interactions

- **Hero sequence:** show a sale entering the POS, highlight its place in a sales summary, then reveal the owner's question and answer. Keep the first meaningful product view visible immediately; animation enhances the explanation.
- **Connected workflow:** as the visitor scrolls, animate the connecting line through records, comparison, consultation, and next step. Use normal page scrolling with short transitions; preserve the story in the reduced-motion version.
- **Question explorer:** provide a small set of selectable business questions. Update the scripted answer and chart together, using matching sample datasets. Keep each transition smooth and readable.
- **Business context selector:** let visitors switch between café, barber, and food-business examples. Change the example vocabulary and illustration to match the selected business. Use an underlined text selector rather than three boxed feature tiles.
- **Useful microinteractions:** highlight the corresponding chart series when its explanation is selected, animate a checklist item into place, and provide clear hover and focus feedback on navigation and CTAs.
- **Customer and founder moments:** use candid business photography, oversized quotes after approval, and a flowing March 2024-to-present timeline with real milestones. Keep these sections visually different from the product demonstration.

Use short, deliberate motion with a clear resting state. Keep touch and keyboard interactions equivalent, respect reduced-motion settings, and pause decorative loops when off-screen. Make the experience delightful without delaying the answer or the CTA.

### Avoid

- **Eyebrows:** no small uppercase pre-headings, decorative overlines, category pills, or badge clusters above section titles. Start each section with its actual heading. Place necessary factual status in the relevant preview caption.
- **Hedging:** remove “might,” “could,” “perhaps,” “potentially,” “we hope,” “designed to help,” and equivalent tentative marketing phrases. State the task and outcome directly; use factual development status for availability.
- **Too many cards:** do not build the page from repeated three-column grids, boxed benefit tiles, or a generic bento layout. Use continuous scenes, typography, dividers, full-width bands, and open compositions. Reserve containers for actual product UI, charts, and inputs that need grouping.
- **Boring repetition:** avoid identical heading/body/card sections, uniformly centered layouts, the same entrance animation everywhere, and static screenshot stacks.
- **Decorative AI clichés:** omit robot mascots, unrelated neural-network graphics, floating technology logos, and sparkle effects that do not explain the workflow.
- **Excessive preview labels:** do not label every screen, chart, answer, and control “mock,” “concept,” or “sample.” One clear caption establishes the context for each complete AI demonstration. Keep the AI availability answer in the FAQ concise. Add context to a standalone shared preview only when its original caption is absent.
- **Obstructive motion:** no scroll hijacking, autoplay sound, prolonged loading intros, flashing effects, or motion that makes data harder to read.

### Mobile and accessibility

On mobile, stack the hero copy above the preview and adapt the connecting story vertically. Keep the answer, chart labels, and CTAs readable at 360 pixels. Provide visible keyboard focus, labeled inputs, accessible errors, a complete reduced-motion presentation, and no horizontal overflow. Use real business photography and customer assets with publication permission.
